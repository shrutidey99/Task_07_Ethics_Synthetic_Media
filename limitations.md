# Limitations and Residual Risk

The University Career Services Synthetic Media Policy reduces foreseeable risk, but it cannot guarantee that synthetic content will remain accurate, identifiable, consensual, or correctly interpreted after publication. Its controls depend on technology, institutional capacity, and human behavior. The following limitations should remain visible to anyone adopting or enforcing the policy.

## 1. Disclosure Can Be Removed or Ignored

The policy requires disclosure inside the media and in surrounding text, but downstream users can crop watermarks, trim opening statements, replace captions, screen-record content, or repost excerpts. A persistent label makes removal harder, not impossible. Even when disclosure survives, audiences may misunderstand “AI-generated,” overlook the notice, or assume that disclosed content is automatically accurate and approved.

Residual risk remains that Career Services content will circulate without its original disclosure or context. Official-channel publication and retained records can help establish the authentic source, but they cannot control every copy.

## 2. Provenance Does Not Establish Truth

Content Credentials and other provenance mechanisms can document who created or modified a file. They cannot prove that the script is accurate, ethically acceptable, or properly consented to. A legitimately signed university file could still contain an error. Credentials may also be stripped through unsupported editing, re-encoding, or screen recording.

The policy therefore treats provenance as evidence of origin, not a truth certificate. This leaves a verification gap for authentic files whose metadata has disappeared and for misleading files copied from legitimate sources.

## 3. Detection Will Produce Uncertainty

Task 6 demonstrated this limitation directly: Deepware Scanner returned inconclusive results for two videos known to be fully synthetic. Detectors may miss generated media, falsely flag authentic media, or provide results without useful explanation. Performance may vary across generators, formats, languages, compression levels, and clip lengths.

The policy correctly avoids using detection as the sole decision-maker, but human investigation can also be slow and uncertain. During that delay, misleading content may continue to spread.

## 4. Consent May Not Be Fully Voluntary

Written consent does not eliminate power differences. An employee may agree because a supervisor requested participation. A student worker may fear that refusal could affect future opportunities even when the policy prohibits retaliation. People may also underestimate how realistic, reusable, or difficult to withdraw a synthetic model will become.

Separate consent, defined uses, expiration dates, and non-synthetic alternatives reduce this risk but cannot prove that an individual felt completely free to refuse. Content already copied by third parties may remain available after consent is revoked.

## 5. Boundaries Will Be Misread

Well-intentioned employees may misunderstand the distinction between ordinary editing and synthetic representation. They may treat voice generation as accessibility support, assume a generic avatar is automatically low-risk, or believe that public information does not require careful review. Contractors may use tools or workflows that internal staff cannot see.

Training and tiered approval help, but a policy cannot anticipate every feature or production method. New tools may combine translation, dubbing, avatar generation, personalization, and analytics in ways that do not fit neatly into existing categories.

## 6. Review Can Become Procedural Rather Than Substantive

Approval requirements can degrade into box-checking, especially when Career Services faces deadlines or high content volume. A named human owner may approve a script without fully checking it. Privacy or legal reviewers may focus on formal compliance while missing how students will interpret the message. Repeated low-risk projects may receive less attention over time.

The audit requirement creates accountability but reviews only a subset of archived content. It may identify problems after publication rather than prevent them.

## 7. Personalization Creates Data and Interpretation Risks

Even authorized personalization can expose student information, reproduce incorrect records, or imply that Career Services has made an individualized professional judgment. A student may treat a generated message as tailored advice when it was produced from limited data and standardized rules.

The policy prohibits high-stakes individualized synthetic guidance, but the line between general information and individualized guidance can become unclear. Mentioning a student's program, interests, or job-search status may make a generic message feel more authoritative than intended.

## 8. Official Content Can Be Repurposed in Bad Faith

A malicious actor can reuse an approved presenter, imitate its visual style, splice authentic segments into fabricated content, or create a false account resembling an official channel. The policy controls Career Services personnel and vendors; it does not bind outside actors.

Publication records, consistent branding, incident response, and prompt corrections help the university establish what is authentic. They cannot prevent every imitation or guarantee removal across all platforms.

## 9. The Policy Depends on Resources

Meaningful review requires staff time, subject-matter expertise, privacy and security support, accessible alternatives, recordkeeping, monitoring, and incident-response capacity. A resource-constrained office may delay reviews, rely heavily on vendors, or fail to monitor content after publication.

If the university cannot supply these resources, the responsible response is to narrow or suspend synthetic-media use rather than weaken the controls. The policy's refusal provisions are only effective if leadership supports employees who say no.

## 10. Bad-Faith Actors Will Ignore the Policy

An employee, contractor, or outsider acting intentionally may bypass approval, conceal prompts, use a personal account, strip disclosures, or move files to an unapproved service. No written policy can technically prevent every deliberate violation.

Access controls, logs, audits, contractual remedies, and discipline increase the cost of misconduct. They do not eliminate it. The policy necessarily relies on some degree of good faith, as all organizational policies do.

## Residual-Risk Position

The policy does not make synthetic media safe by default. It establishes a presumption of restraint: use the least realistic format that meets the communication need, preserve human accountability, refuse prohibited and high-stakes uses, and maintain the ability to correct or withdraw content.

Residual risk is acceptable only when the communication benefit is specific, the script is verifiable, no person's identity is exploited, disclosure is meaningful, data use is limited, and a responsible employee can intervene. When those conditions are absent—or when Career Services cannot confidently explain why synthetic representation is necessary—the office should not produce the content.

The policy's most honest limitation is that it cannot restore trust after every failure. Its purpose is therefore not to promise perfect prevention. It is to create clear boundaries, make responsible decisions traceable, and ensure that convenience or novelty never substitutes for accountability.
